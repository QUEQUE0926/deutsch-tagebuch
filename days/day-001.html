📖 Full translation · English (translate it yourself first, then check)

¶1 — My name is Tom. I come from China and I have been living in Berlin for three days. I am an engineer. I work at a company in Mitte.

¶2 — This evening I am going to a meetup. The meetup is called "Sprachen lernen in Berlin" (Learning languages in Berlin). There are many people there from many countries: from Spain, from Italy, from Brazil and from Germany.

¶3 — A woman is called Anna. She comes from Munich, but she has lived in Berlin for a long time. She is nice. She speaks German slowly with me. That is good for me.

¶4 — Anna says: "Berlin is a big city. Many people from all over the world live here." I think that's great.

¶5 — I am learning German because I want to work in Germany. The language is not easy, but I practice every day. Anna gives me her number. We are meeting at the weekend. She will show me the city.

¶6 — Berlin is now my new home. I am happy here.

🧩 Grammar in context · 3 points from today's text

"Heute Abend gehe ich zu einem Meetup."

1 · Verb-second (V2) word order — The conjugated verb ALWAYS sits in slot 2. Here the time phrase "Heute Abend" takes slot 1, so the verb gehe moves right behind it. Statements and yes/no questions both obey this rule: "Ich komme aus China" → "Kommst du aus China?"

"Sie kommt aus München, aber sie wohnt schon lange in Berlin."

2 · Present-tense endings — Regular verbs add -e / -st / -t: ich komme, du kommst, sie kommt; ich wohne, du wohnst, sie wohnt. In the dialogue: ich heiße, du heißst. The one big exception is sein: ich bin, du bist, sie ist.

"Ich komme aus China und ich wohne in Berlin."

3 · aus + Dativ (origin) vs. in + Dativ (location) — aus answers "where from?" (origin, nationality): aus China, aus München. in answers "where do you live?" (current location): in Berlin, in Mitte. Both take Dativ — country names usually drop the article, cities never take one.

小舌 R (as in kommen 🔊)Reading R as Chinese "日" (rì) or as English "l"First say a soft English "h" to feel throat vibration; then add voice — that friction is the uvular [ʁ].
两种标准变体Forcing a hard trill everywhereTwo equally standard options: ① uvular (back of mouth, "French R" style, north/standard German); ② tongue-tip trill (south Germany & Austria). Pick ONE and stick with it.
