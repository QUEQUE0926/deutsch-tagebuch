Grammar Tap ① · "Wie heißt du?" — The Verb-Second (V2) rule

Layer 1 · Sentence breakdown

Wie | heißt | du? How | are-called | you? [Q-word] [VERB slot 2] [Subject]

In German, the conjugated verb ALWAYS sits in position 2 — in both statements AND questions. Here "heißt" is the verb, occupying slot 2 right after the question word "Wie".

Layer 2 · Word annotation

  • wie — question word, "how"
  • heißt — verb, 2nd person singular of heißen, "are called" (the -st ending marks "du")
  • du — pronoun, "you" (informal, singular)

Layer 3 · Study tip · Why V2 matters

German word order feels strange to English speakers because English lets the verb float. In German the verb is anchored to slot 2 — like a button on a shirt that has to be in the second hole from the top. Everything else can shuffle around it.

Statement: Ich komme aus China. (Subject–Verb–Rest)
Question: Woher kommst du? (Q-word–Verb–Subject) — same verb slot, different ordering of the others.
Grammar Tap ② · "Ich komme aus China." — Preposition aus + Dativ

Layer 1 · Sentence breakdown

Ich | komme | aus | China. I | come | from | China. [Subject] [Verb slot 2] [Preposition] [Noun]

Layer 2 · Word annotation

  • ich — 1st-person singular pronoun, "I"
  • komme — 1st-person singular of kommen, "come"
  • aus — preposition, "from / out of"; always takes the Dativ case
  • China — proper noun, neuter country name; no article with most countries

Layer 3 · Study tip · aus vs von

Both can mean "from", but they're not interchangeable:

  • aus = origin (born there, nationality): Ich komme aus China.
  • von = from a person or a place you visited: Ich komme vom Arzt. (from the doctor's office)
Country names without articles: aus China, aus Deutschland, aus Japan.
Countries WITH articles need them: aus der Schweiz (f.), aus den USA (pl.), aus dem Iran (m.).

Quiz (3 questions · stems in English · A1 style)

Q1 · Information location — Where does Tom work?

  • A) At a company in Mitte
  • B) At a Meetup in Kreuzberg
  • C) In China
Show answer & explanation
Answer: A
Tom writes "Ich arbeite bei einer Firma in Mitte." → He works at a company in Mitte (a central Berlin district). Kreuzberg is mentioned only as where his new friend Paul lives; China is his country of origin, not his workplace.
If you got this wrong, classify yourself: ☐ 单词不认识 (didn't know Firma / Mitte) ☐ 语法没懂 (didn't parse the sentence) ☐ 理解偏了 (mixed up locations) ☐ 粗心 (careless)

Q2 · True or False — Anna is originally from Berlin.

Show answer & explanation
Answer: False
The text says "Sie kommt aus München, aber sie wohnt schon lange in Berlin." → Anna comes from Munich (München), but has lived in Berlin for a long time. "aus München" tells us her origin is Munich, not Berlin.
If you got this wrong, classify yourself: ☐ 单词不认识 (didn't know München / wohnt) ☐ 语法没懂 (missed the aus + aber contrast) ☐ 理解偏了 (conflated origin with current residence) ☐ 粗心 (careless)

Q3 · Multiple choice — Why is Tom learning German?

  • A) Because Anna told him to
  • B) Because he wants to work in Germany
  • C) Because German is easy
Show answer & explanation
Answer: B
Tom says "Ich lerne Deutsch, weil ich in Deutschland arbeiten möchte." → He learns German because he wants to work in Germany. Note the subclause introduced by weil ("because") pushes the verb möchte to the very end — a teaser for Day 91+.
If you got this wrong, classify yourself: ☐ 单词不认识 (didn't know möchte / weil) ☐ 语法没懂 (didn't catch the weil clause) ☐ 理解偏了 (picked the easier-sounding option) ☐ 粗心 (careless)

Day 1 · Pronunciation self-check · 小舌音 R (the German R)

This is the sound most Chinese speakers get wrong on Day 1 — and that's fine, you have 179 more days to refine it.

Wrong waySelf-check action
Reading R as Chinese "日" (rì) or "勒" (lē)First say a soft English "h" to feel throat vibration; then add voice — you'll get the uvular fricative [ʁ].
Forcing a hard trill everywhereTwo equally-standard options exist: ① uvular (back of mouth, the "French R" style, common in north/standard German); ② tongue-tip (alveolar trill, like Italian/Spanish "r", common in south Germany & Austria). Pick ONE and stick with it.

Tip: In everyday German, the R at the END of a syllable often softens to a quiet [ɐ] — almost like the "a" in English "sofa". So "der" often sounds like "de-a", not "derrr". Don't roll it there.
